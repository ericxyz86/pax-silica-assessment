How does the record compare with the §23.3 decision rule?

The rule’s first branch covers any binding instrument. The assessment states: “The Philippines should not sign any instrument that grants land possession or exclusive use; fixes a long lease term; leaves rent undetermined; restricts Philippine jurisdiction or regulatory authority; grants any immunity; commits public infrastructure or fiscal incentives; creates mineral-supply obligations; permits subleasing, transfer or collateralisation of any land right; or gives any investor or foreign government preferred rights — unless all seven preconditions at §23.1 have first been satisfied and the red lines at §23.2 are respected in the text.” Only one part of this branch can be applied to the record now. As of 14 November 2026, 0 of 7 preconditions are met, so on the record the first condition of the branch’s exception is not satisfied. Two questions cannot be evaluated without a text: whether the contemplated instrument grants any of the enumerated rights, and whether it respects the red lines. No text or draft of the instrument was located in the assessment record as of 14 November 2026. Accounts of the instrument have accumulated: BCDA’s reply of 13 October; the Palace’s description on 4 November of a memorandum that is not final; Rodolfo’s “The master agreement itself, the whole thing” on 10 November; and BCDA’s statement to the House committees on 11 November that Philippine laws will govern. The assessment records that this last statement does not establish that the United States accepts exclusive Philippine jurisdiction. It also records that none of these accounts supplies the negotiated text.

The second branch covers a strictly non-binding framework. The rule states that signing one is acceptable “only if the text itself expressly states that it creates no lease or land right; confers no investor entitlement or exclusivity; commits no public funding or incentives; leaves every project subject to ordinary Philippine law, permits and approvals; permits either government to withdraw; and subjects every subsequent binding commercial instrument to separate approval and public disclosure. A framework that is silent on these points should be treated as branch one.” Neither the reported descriptions nor the relayed messages are a text. Three text-dependent questions therefore remain open: what rights, if any, the instrument creates and for whom; whether it contains the express non-binding terms; and whether it respects the red lines. None can be evaluated without a located text. The assessment puts this question to whatever is tabled: “what rights, if any, does this text create, and for whom?” It adds: “If the answer is ‘none, expressly,’ the signature is legally a photograph, although political lock-in may still follow (KJ-16). If the answer is anything else, the seven preconditions apply in full.”

The assessment’s own stated recommendation is: “The recommendation is neither endorsement nor rejection. The Philippines should not sign an instrument creating substantive rights before the required conditions are met; a strictly non-binding framework is acceptable only with every express safeguard of §23.3.”
