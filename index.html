The maths proves something narrow, but decisive. BCDA's plan to catch the water on the site itself fails at the top of BCDA's own demand estimate. At 65 million litres a day, with good rainfall, catching it on site is arguable. At 90 million, it is not. What taking that water would do to people downstream is a separate question, and the maths cannot answer it. Holding water back could capture rainy-season flow that currently runs to the sea unused. It could equally cut the supply people depend on in the dry months. Telling those two apart needs a study of the whole river system, including how much water it has to keep flowing to stay healthy. Her preferred configuration is a stand-alone system with its own generation, renewable or gas. The interview puts the load at about 19% of Luzon’s projected 2026 peak, matching this report’s figure by an independent route, and notes it is close to the entire Visayas grid. The 1,300 MW gap is what is left after counting 500 MW of ACWA solar and the proposed 1,200 MW of LNG against the 3,000 MW required. And that is before allowing for the obvious: a 500 MW solar plant does not produce 500 MW at night. The LNG carries a logistics problem of its own: it arrives by ship, but Clark has an airport, not a seaport, so the fuel must land at a coastal terminal and travel inland by a pipeline that does not exist. That is what the unsolicited Subic–Clark fuel-line proposal is for. Set against BCDA’s Q1 2028 site-development target, none of these timelines fit. What changes here is not the direction of the finding but its standing: the department that would have to approve grid connection has said publicly that it will not approve it at scale.

Benchmark: BCDA's own signed ACWA Power lease, 500 hectares for up to 500 MW of solar plus storage, i.e. 1 ha per MW of nameplate. Solar at 20% capacity factor. The Department of Energy projects Luzon's 2026 peak demand at 15,600 MW. The highest peak actually recorded by mid-2026 was 14,534 MW. So a 3 GW load is 19.2% of the projection, and 20.6% of the real maximum. On 15 May 2026 the grid ran a 373 MW deficit under red alert with 4,106 MW on forced outage across 30 plants. The US DFC has funded a feasibility study for a ≥1,200 MW LNG terminal and plant at the site.

11

frequently asked questions

Direct answers, condensed from the assessment. Every figure is traceable to the report sections on this page.

What is Pax Silica?

Pax Silica is a United States-led initiative — a programme of the US State Department, not an international organisation — coordinating what it calls trusted supply chains for semiconductors, AI infrastructure, critical minerals and energy. Its founding Declaration is deliberately non-binding: it creates no obligations, no financing and no enforceable benefits. Pax Silica has 25 signatories; Taiwan endorses its principles without signing.

What is proposed for New Clark City?

The Philippines has publicly offered approximately 1,618.74 hectares of state land — 17.1% of New Clark City, in Capas and Bamban, Tarlac — to host the initiative's first "Economic Security Zone": a semiconductor, AI-infrastructure and advanced-manufacturing district with a named but uncommitted anchor tenant. No survey, technical description or map annex of the parcel has been published.

Is anything signed or legally binding yet?

No. What exists is a non-binding declaration signed on 16 April 2026, a four-page BCDA proposal letter, a named but uncommitted anchor tenant, and roughly thirty unnamed letters of interest. Nothing about the project is legally binding, financed, contracted or permitted at the project level, and fifteen of sixteen implementation gates are open.

Is this assessment for or against the project?

Neither. The assessment endorses nothing and rejects nothing. Its recommendation is that the Philippines should decline to sign in November 2026 unless seven defined preconditions — disclosures and binding conditions, all within the executive's control — are satisfied first. None is met as at the latest verification.

What are the seven preconditions?

(1) The parcel legally defined and Annex A published; (2) the draft framework published with a public comment period; (3) independent water-balance and grid-adequacy studies published; (4) Year-3 rent and the lease term determined against an independent land valuation; (5) DFA-OLA and OSG legal opinions published; (6) the NCIP certification precondition process formally initiated; (7) the activity mix and underlying economic studies published. If any one is unmet in November, the assessment's recommendation is not to sign.

What are the biggest unresolved issues?

Water and power are the binding physical constraints: harvesting the stated 90 MLD from rainfall would need a catchment of between 90% and 193% of the entire site, and the 3 GW power figure is about a fifth of Luzon's peak demand, on a grid that ran a 373 MW deficit in May 2026. Separately, no published model reconciles US$40–70 billion of investment with 130,000–190,000 direct jobs.

Why does November 2026 matter?

Both governments hope to sign a framework agreement during the ASEAN Summit in November 2026. The assessment's decision rule: a genuinely non-binding framework that expressly creates no rights leaves the signature as a photograph; any instrument that grants land, fixes terms or creates entitlements before the seven preconditions are met crosses the red line — substantive rights before due diligence is complete.

Who publishes this, and how is it kept current?

Agile Intelligence publishes it. The site is operated by AI systems under human editorial direction: institutional sources and Philippine media are swept every morning, and nothing on this site changes until the underlying claim has passed an adversarial verification step performed by a second, independent AI model. Every change is versioned and logged; corrections are stated as corrections. Final editorial authority, and the decision to publish, rest with the human publisher.
